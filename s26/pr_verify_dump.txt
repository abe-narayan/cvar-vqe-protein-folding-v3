[notes] SPOKEN:
Why are short peptides hard? Three reasons I can measure. First, the sequence says almost nothing about the backbone at this length. Predicting the phi angle from the full sequence context gives a mean error of 36.1 degrees over 1,507 residues. A model that ignores the sequence and uses only the corpus average gives 36.4 degrees. That is the whole sequence signal for phi: a third of a degree. Second, the candidates I retrieve all share the same mistake. 68% of the pool's error is common to every member, so averaging cannot remove it. Third, the only steep lever is the distance prior itself. Interpolating the prior toward the true distances moves the answer by -2.15 angstroms per unit of progress, on the point-cloud basis, and 2.2% of the way would reach three angstroms. That number is an oracle. It says where the accuracy lives, not how to get it. For scale, a constant alpha-helix, which knows nothing about the sequence, scores 4.06 angstroms on the built chain. The whole pipeline is 0.85 angstroms better than that. Everything is measured on 126 cluster-disjoint targets in five pinned folds, paired per target, with a minimum detectable effect of 2.8016 standard errors computed per comparison, and a fold-clustered confidence interval next to every mean.

[notes] SPOKEN:
Here is the pipeline. The sequence goes through ESM-2 into a distogram: a seventeen-bin posterior over every pairwise CA distance, trained leave-fold-out. In parallel, BLOSUM62 retrieval pulls five hundred real protein windows of the right length from a library of 787 peptides and 6,003 fragments. Each window is scored by its Bayes risk against the posterior, and the best seventy-five are kept. The quantum stage can select a subset of the top one hundred and twenty-eight, but it is off in production; the 3.2148 result never passes through it. The kept windows are averaged in coordinate space. That average scores 3.0483 angstroms, but it is not a structure: its virtual CA-CA bonds are 2.96 angstroms on average, with a worst case of 0.65. So the average is projected onto a chain with ideal geometry. That built chain, at 3.2148 angstroms, is the result I report; the projection costs 0.166 angstroms and I pay it because the chain is a real structure. An optional AMBER relaxation gives 3.2355. I will show later that it is a validity step, not an accuracy step. One design fact matters for everything that follows. The output is an average, so it tracks the whole set, not its best member. A selector that can only remove members from a ranked list cannot move it much.

[notes] SPOKEN:
The quantum component is a real CVaR-VQE, and I checked it before I measured it. It is a 7-qubit, 3-layer RY and CNOT circuit with 21 parameters, simulated as an exact statevector over 128 candidate structures. Against a dense simulator written independently from the gate list, the probabilities agree to 5.551e-17. The gradient is the exact parameter-shift rule; against finite differences its cosine is 1.000000000 with relative error 4.597e-10. Nothing is sampled in the trained path. The Hamiltonian is diagonal: the rank-standardised scores of the candidates. Because the candidates are already sorted, it is the same ladder on every target to within 1.2% of its range. I proved that the CVaR tail is always a subset of a prefix of that ranking: 2,592 adversarial cells, 0 violations. So the circuit can delete a candidate from the classical top set; it can never add one. Then I measured. The optimiser does train: it beats the best of 200 untrained draws and closes 78% to 89% of the free-energy gap. It stops 0.902 nats from its own Gibbs optimum, and the endpoint cannot tell: 0.24 of the minimum detectable effect. Against the plain argmin the selector is -0.1405 angstroms at 0.68 of its MDE, on the selection basis. By my own rule that is not a result. I claim no quantum advantage.
